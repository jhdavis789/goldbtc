Stock-to-Flow (S2F)
"PlanB" (pseudonymous)
 Scarcity ratio 2021-cycle model called ~$100k–$288k; high six figures on scarcity alone Regresses price on the stock-to-flow ratio (existing supply ÷ annual new issuance). Each halving doubles S2F, producing step-changes in modeled value. The S2FX variant places BTC on a cross-asset scarcity line with gold and silver. A1Gold market-share TAMstore of value
Value Bitcoin as a percentage of gold's market cap, on the premise that BTC is "digital gold" chasing the same store-of-value demand.
How it works. Take gold's market cap, or just its investment slice (ETFs, bars, coins, central-bank holdings — roughly $18–30T depending on year and definition). Pick a penetration rate: the share of that market BTC eventually captures (10%, 25%, 50%, or full parity). Multiply to get an implied BTC market cap, then divide by circulating (or fixed 21M) supply for a per-coin price. The entire model reduces to one judgment: what share of gold does BTC take, and by when.
Pros
  • One transparent assumption — easy to communicate and stress-test
  • Anchored to a real, liquid comparable with a known size
  • Captures the "digital gold" thesis most institutions actually hold
Cons
  • Entirely hostage to the penetration % — no theory pins it
  • Assumes BTC and gold are substitutes; gold's own cap moves too
  • Silent on timing; circular if gold is itself mispriced

A2Multi-market / store-of-value TAM build-upstore of value
Sum Bitcoin's projected capture across many addressable pools — gold, bonds, M2, real estate, FX reserves, treasuries — then divide by supply.
How it works. Enumerate discrete global asset/monetary pools and their sizes. Assign each an independent penetration rate (often bear/base/bull or probability-weighted) for a target year. Multiply size × penetration per pool, sum the captured dollars into a total network value, divide by projected circulating (or active) supply → price per coin. It's A1 generalized across many comparables; the output is only as credible as the weakest penetration input.
Pros
  • Richer than single-asset gold-share; captures BTC's multiple roles
  • Forces explicit, auditable assumptions pool-by-pool
  • Naturally yields bear/base/bull bands
Cons
  • Many free parameters → can be tuned to any target
  • Double-counting risk across overlapping pools
  • Compounding uncertainty; penetration rates are asserted, not derived

A3Volatility-adjusted gold parityvol-adj gold
Compute what BTC is worth at parity with gold's investment market, then discount it by how much more volatile BTC is — equal risk capital, not equal dollars.
How it works. Start from the private gold investment market (~$5–8T: ETFs, bars, coins). A dollar in BTC carries more risk than a dollar in gold, so divide the gold base by the BTC/gold volatility ratio (~1.5–2× recently, historically 4–5×). The result is the BTC market cap that consumes the same risk capital as gold; ÷ supply = fair value. As BTC's volatility falls toward gold's, the implied fair value mechanically rises.
Pros
  • Risk-adjusted — compares the two on equal-risk terms, not naive dollars
  • Mechanical and falsifiable; few discretionary inputs
  • Explains why fair value rises as BTC matures (vol compression)
Cons
  • Two moving inputs (gold base + vol ratio) → target swings a lot
  • Volatility ratio is backward-looking and unstable
  • Still assumes gold is the right comparable

A4Monetary model — medium-of-exchange + reservemonetary
Value BTC as future money — a share of global trade settlement plus a share of central-bank reserves — sized through the velocity of money.
How it works. Project the transactional volume BTC will settle (e.g. X% of international + domestic trade by 2050). Via MV=PQ, the monetary base needed to support that throughput is PQ/V, where V is BTC's velocity (turnover). Add a reserve-asset slice (BTC as Y% of central-bank balance sheets) and residual store-of-value demand. Sum the implied monetary base, divide by effective supply → price. Decades of macro growth and debasement scale the throughput up.
Pros
  • Grounded in monetary economics, not just analogy
  • Captures BTC's "money" role, not only store-of-value
  • Velocity makes the model honest about turnover vs hoarding
Cons
  • Velocity is nearly impossible to estimate yet dominates the answer
  • 2050 horizon → enormous compounding error
  • Assumes transactional adoption that may never materialize

A5Equation of exchange (MV=PQ) discounted-utilitymonetary
The academic core of the monetary model: solve M = PQ/V for the network's required monetary base, then discount future utility back to today.
How it works. Treat BTC as money for a defined economy. Estimate the economy's transactional value PQ (price × quantity of the resource it provisions), estimate velocity V, solve for the monetary base M = PQ/V; per-coin value is M ÷ supply. Future-year M is discounted to present value at a high early-stage rate (~30–50%). It is orthogonal to scarcity — high velocity can yield a LOW value even with fixed supply.
Pros
  • The rigorous first-principles version of "BTC as money"
  • Makes velocity and discount rate explicit, not hidden
  • Shows why fixed supply alone doesn't guarantee a high price
Cons
  • Outputs swing wildly with velocity and discount assumptions
  • Hard to define PQ for a non-cash-flow asset
  • Academic — rarely produces a single investable target

A6Network adoption — Metcalfe + S-curvenetwork
Value scales with the square of the user base (Metcalfe's Law), and the user base grows along a technology-adoption S-curve.
How it works. Model adoption (active addresses / users) as an S-curve calibrated to historical tech diffusion (internet, mobile). Convert users to value via Metcalfe's Law — network value ∝ users² — fitted as a regression on price history. Where you sit on the S-curve, and how steep it is, sets the trajectory. Often overlaid with macro factors (real rates, money supply).
Pros
  • Grounded in a real, observed driver (network effects) with strong fit
  • Produces a trajectory, not just a point
  • Ties value to something measurable (on-chain users)
Cons
  • "Users" is gameable/ambiguous (addresses ≠ people; exchanges custody many)
  • Metcalfe fits can be overfit; the exponent is contested
  • S-curve ceiling and slope are assumptions

A7Power-law / log-linear time regressiontrend / regression
Fit price to a power law (or exponential) in time-since-genesis and extrapolate the trendline forward.
How it works. Plot price vs time on log-log (power law) or log-linear (constant CAGR) axes and fit a regression. The Bitcoin Power Law finds log(price) ∝ ~5.8·log(time), giving a long-run corridor; log-linear variants fit a constant compound growth (~doubling/yr) and project it forward. Time is the only input — no fundamentals.
Pros
  • Remarkably tight 15-year fit with few parameters
  • Gives a corridor with support/resistance, not a single guess
  • Hard to manipulate — just price and time
Cons
  • Pure curve-fitting; no causal mechanism
  • Assumes the past trajectory simply continues — breaks at saturation
  • Exponent / trendline drift over time

A8Scarcity / stock-to-flowscarcity
Regress price on the stock-to-flow ratio — existing supply ÷ annual new issuance — so each halving mechanically lifts modeled value.
How it works. Compute S2F = circulating supply ÷ annual production. Regress (log) market value on (log) S2F across history; the fitted line gives a "fair value" that steps up at each halving, when issuance halves and S2F doubles. The S2FX variant places BTC on a cross-asset scarcity line with gold and silver as "phase transitions."
Pros
  • Captures BTC's defining feature (programmed scarcity) in one number
  • Halving schedule is known, so it's forward-projectable
  • Was strikingly accurate 2012–2020
Cons
  • Demand-blind — models only supply; broke badly after 2021
  • Spurious-regression critique (two trending series)
  • Treats scarcity as sufficient for value, which it isn't

A9Supply/demand flow imbalanceflow / market structure
Compare incoming demand (ETFs, treasuries, sovereigns) against new issuance; when demand persistently exceeds the trickle of new coins, price must clear higher.
How it works. Quantify new supply (~165k BTC/yr post-2024 halving) and demand flows (ETF net inflows, corporate/sovereign treasury buys). When buyers absorb >100% of new issuance, marginal coins come only from existing holders willing to sell; the thesis is those sellers "run out" → seller exhaustion → repricing. A market-microstructure argument, not a fair-value calc.
Pros
  • Directly observable, near-real-time data (ETF flows, issuance)
  • Strong near-term causal mechanism
  • Intuitive: fixed new supply vs growing institutional demand
Cons
  • Ignores elastic supply from the ~20M existing coins (dormant can wake)
  • No price target — says "up," not "to what"
  • Flows reverse; outflows break the thesis (as StanChart found)

A12Portfolio risk-budgeting / allocation frameworkallocation
Skip the price target entirely — decide how big a sleeve BTC should be given its volatility and correlation (or set it to zero).
How it works. Treat sizing, not valuation, as the question. Measure BTC's volatility and correlation to the rest of the book; size the allocation so it contributes an acceptable share of total portfolio risk (BlackRock: 1–2% ≈ the risk of one Magnificent-7 stock; beyond ~2% the risk share balloons). A14Cost-of-production / energy-value floorcost / floor
Treat the marginal cost of mining a coin — mostly electricity — as a soft price floor.
How it works. Estimate the all-in cost to mine one BTC (fleet efficiency × power price × kWh, plus hardware depreciation), or convert cumulative network energy into an "energy value."   • Causality debated (price may drive cost, not vice versa)

A16Skeptic / no-intrinsic-valueskeptic
The anti-valuation stance — with no cash flows or intrinsic value, BTC can't be valued and has no place in a long-term portfolio.
How it works. Apply a traditional investability test: an asset must produce cash flows or have intrinsic/utility value to be valued and held strategically. Reading the table. The cleanest divide is methodological, not numerical. BlackRock (and, for clients, Fidelity) answers the allocation question — "how big a sleeve, sized for risk" — rather than naming a price. Everyone who does commit to a number uses one of a few engines — addressable-market build-ups (ARK use-case TAMs, VanEck's monetary model, Bitwise/Grayscale/WisdomTree/SSGA store-of-value share of gold), network-adoption math (Fidelity's Timmer), and a volatility-adjusted gold-parity formula (JPMorgan). Gold is the shared anchor for most — used three ways: a TAM to capture a share of, a qualitative debasement narrative, or a precise risk-capital divisor.

Several widely-quoted numbers are individual, not the firm's house view: Fidelity's ~$1M/2030 is Timmer's personal model (FDAS itself commits to no target); Galaxy's "BTC overtakes gold" is Novogratz's CEO remark, separate from Thorn's $250k/2027 research; Fink's $500–700k is an offhand Davos hypothetical.
